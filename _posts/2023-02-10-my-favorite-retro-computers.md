## Pegasus: Family Game

My parents gave me my first game console around Christmas 1994. It was a Pegasus IQ-502, a Nintendo Entertainment System clone made by the Taiwanese company Micro Genius. Pegasus consoles became hugely popular in Poland in the early 90s, and for many of us, they were our introduction to video games.

Inside was a UA6527P processor running at 1.77 MHz, a counterpart to the MOS 6502-based Ricoh chips found in Nintendo’s Famicom and NES. The system had just 2 KB of RAM and another 2 KB of video RAM. Tiny amounts by today’s standards, but enough to bring entire worlds to our living-room TV.

In one practical respect, the Pegasus even had an advantage over the original Famicom: it offered composite video output, whereas Nintendo’s original model relied solely on RF. In theory, that meant a cleaner picture. In practice, I couldn’t see any difference between the two connections on our family’s Panasonic CRT. I eventually left the Pegasus connected through our VCR’s RF input, which made it easier to keep everything hooked up without constantly swapping cables.

For all its modest graphics and sound capabilities, this little 8-bit machine had one feature that made a real difference in platform games: smooth hardware scrolling. Compared with machines such as the original MSX, it could make moving through a game world feel remarkably fluid.

![Pegasus IQ-502]({{site.url}}/assets/2023-02-10/Pegasus_IQ-502.webp)

My Famicom cartridge collection isn’t huge, but I’ve managed to find almost all my childhood favorites. Unfortunately, none of my original cartridges survived, but they were at least findable on eBay. 😄

My all-time favorites are **Super Mario Bros.**, the first game I ever played on a console, and **Tiny Toon Adventures**, which was on the very first cartridge I bought with my mom. I also have a few Chinese “reproductions” from AliExpress, similar to the cartridges we could buy in Polish shops in the early ’90s. Well, maybe today they have a slightly more games.

## My very first personal computer

As a child, I thought home computers had more to offer than consoles: they had keyboards, and you could program them. My first personal computer was an **Atari 800XL**, which my uncle gave me after my cousins upgraded to a Commodore Amiga 500. Their old 8-bit machine had been collecting dust, but for me, it opened up a whole new world.

Since I had no software for it, I had to write my own programs to get any enjoyment out of it. The 800XL was part of Atari’s 8-bit computer family, introduced in 1979. Like the Pegasus, it used a processor from the 6502 family, but it had considerably more RAM: 64 KB. I could also save my programs to tape and load them again later—a significant step up from the Pegasus, where I could only play whatever was on a cartridge.

The main components of the Atari include a MOS 6502 CPU clocked at 1.77 MHz (the PAL version; the NTSC version was slightly faster) and a 10 KB ROM containing the OS. It features two video chips: ANTIC, which supports 8 graphics modes ranging from resolutions of 40×24 to 320×192 pixels in 2 or 4 colors, and GTIA, which offers 11 graphics modes with resolutions from 40×24 to 320×192 pixels in 2, 4, or 16 colors. Impressively, it can display up to 256 colors on the screen.

![Atari 800XL]({{site.url}}/assets/2023-02-10/Atari800XL.webp)

When I got my first PC in 2001, I packed away my older machines and stored them in the attic. They stayed there for the next 17 years, until I rediscovered them in 2018.

My Atari still works perfectly to this day. All it needed was a little care: in 2020, I cleaned the keyboard and circuit board with isopropyl alcohol. These days, I connect it to a 14-inch Sanyo CRT TV, or a modern TV through a video converter. Whichever screen I use, there’s something satisfying about returning to the same machine I used as a child.

## Commodore 64

I first experienced the Commodore 64 as an adult. I had neither owned one nor even seen one in person during the 1990s, but I’d heard plenty of stories from people who had. The sheer variety of software and games available for it always fascinated me.

My machine is a Commodore 64C, a later model from the early 1990s that retains compatibility with the original 1982 “breadbin” C64. Inside, it has a revised SID sound chip and a more compact motherboard, with higher-capacity memory chips reducing the number of components needed.

Comparing its graphics capabilities with those of the Atari 800XL isn’t straightforward. The Atari offers a broader color palette and a wider range of graphics modes, while the C64 has a more flexible hardware sprite system—a major advantage for moving characters and objects around the screen. Each machine has its strengths, and which produces better-looking games remains a favorite topic of debate among enthusiasts.

Both computers have 64 KB of RAM and processors from the MOS 6502 family, though their implementations differ. The Atari runs at a higher nominal clock speed, but that alone doesn’t tell the whole story: the custom graphics and sound hardware plays a huge part in what each machine can do.

![Commodore 64]({{site.url}}/assets/2023-02-10/Commodore64.webp)

I love the sound of the SID chip. It has three voices, each capable of producing sawtooth, triangle, pulse, or noise waveforms. Its analog filter helps give it that distinctive character, with subtle differences in sound from one chip to another.

To load programs, I use two devices: a **Tapecart**, which connects to the cassette port, and an **SD2IEC**, which loads files from an SD card through the Commodore’s serial IEC port. The SD2IEC doesn’t fully emulate a Commodore disk drive, so compatibility varies, but many games in D64 disk images work directly or have versions patched for it.

## Atari vs. Commodore

Back in the ’90s, there was a lot of competition between Atari and Commodore users. The ZX Spectrum wasn’t a big part of my childhood, while newer 16-bit and 32-bit machines were only beginning to enter my world. Looking back, though, I find both platforms fascinating.

In my experience, loading programs from tape was more reliable on the Commodore, and loading speeds could be improved through software alone, without modifying the hardware. On the Atari, roughly one in ten attempts would end in a loading error—a frustration I remember well.

Each machine also had its own sound. The Atari’s audio had an arcade-like character that I still enjoy, while the Commodore’s SID chip offered richer possibilities for music, giving many games soundtracks that were memorable in their own right.

## Handhelds & emulation

The only Nintendo handheld I knew about as a child was the Game Boy. Nobody at my school owned one, so we played Pokémon Gold on a PC emulator instead. I didn’t get to play it on a Nintendo handheld until years later, when I bought a Nintendo DSi, almost 14 years after its release. The DSi lets me emulate Game Boy games as well as explore the Nintendo DS library—a platform that sold more than 150 million units across its various models.

![Nintendo DSi]({{site.url}}/assets/2023-02-10/Nintendo_DSi.webp)

Emulation doesn’t quite capture the full experience of using the original hardware, but most of the time, it’s enough for me. There’s no need to dig out a bulky power supply and untangle a pile of cables. Having so many retro systems in my pocket makes it much easier to enjoy them whenever I have a little time to play.

I used to emulate old 8-bit games on my Raspberry Pi 3 Model B+, especially when playing with someone else. These days, though, it’s usually easier to pick up a handheld like the Anbernic RG35XX or Trimui Brick. I own a few of these little devices, and when I reach for one, it’s usually to play Castlevania or Chrono Trigger.

![Anbernic]({{site.url}}/assets/2023-02-10/anbernic.webp)

## Wrapping up

What I love most about these 8-bit machines is how much people can still achieve with so little. Their graphics and sound have clear limits, yet developers continue to create impressive games and demos. Active communities keep both platforms alive, releasing new games and finding clever ways to push the hardware further. After more than 40 years, these computers can still surprise us.

These old machines also mean a lot to me personally. As long as I have room for them, they’re staying. But I’m definitely not expanding the collection. At least, that’s the plan. 😄
